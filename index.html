Early stages of rocket launchers

Initially, early medieval rockets were associated with weaponry, especially gunpowder, during the 1200s, examples included bamboo filed gunpowder poles and Examples included bamboo poles filled with gunpowder. Due to their effectiveness at inflicting the most damage over a large range, they spread all around the world (Needham, 1986; Haw, 2013).

Technological developments and military applications in the 1700–1800s

In the 1700 to 1800s there were more tightly incorporated into the military field. A major field William Congreve pioneered the design of improved rockets based on Mysore rockets (Stephen, 1887). Later, Congreve and Hale, inspired by the previous Mysorean rockets, designed improved heavy military rockets used in the Napoleonic Wars (Wikipedia, 2009). This idea finally came to light when the American physicist Robert Goddard launched the first successful liquid-fuelled rocket. Unlike traditional solid-fuel rockets, the propellants could be stored separately and could theoretically be throttled. Furthermore, a liquid-based fuel system allows for smaller, lighter combustion chambers relative to the energy they produce (NASA, 2021).

Dawn of modern rocketry

Over the early 20th century there was a technological boom in rockets due to World War II and the Cold War (the Space Race). In 1957 the Soviets successfully launched their first artificial satellite into space, proving that liquid-fuelled rockets could be advanced enough to achieve orbital velocity, followed by a human in space on Vostok 1 in April 1961 (NASA; Wikipedia – Sputnik 1 & Vostok 1). Along with these achievements by the Soviets, the US formed NASA and developed the Saturn V rocket—a three-stage liquid-fuelled vehicle standing 363 feet tall, powered by liquid oxygen, kerosene, and liquid hydrogen—and it was the only rocket at the time capable of carrying humans beyond low Earth orbit (NASA, 2010a). Nowadays, at the dawn of the 21st century, NASA increased reusability by operating the partially reusable Space Shuttle from 1981 to 2011. Furthermore, new aerospace companies such as SpaceX and Blue Origin have helped push forward reusable launch vehicles, which have significantly lowered the cost of access to space.
